## Peer-Reviewed Publications

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

[de Kadt, Daniel](https://www.ddekadt.com/), **Ada Johnson-Kanu**, and [Melissa Sands](https://www.melissasands.org/) (2024). [State Violence, Party Formation, and Electoral Accountability: The political legacy of the Marikana massacre](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/state-violence-party-formation-and-electoral-accountability-the-political-legacy-of-the-marikana-massacre/53053BE87AC75FE723B5E447E0AA0A28). *American Political Science Review*, 118(2), 563–583. [Lead article]
<span class="links">[Preprint (PDF)]({{ '/assets/pdf/papers/marikana-apsr.pdf' | relative_url }})</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Democratic governments sometimes use violence against their people, yet little is known about the electoral consequences of these events. Studying South Africa’s Marikana massacre, we document how a new opposition party formed as a direct result of violence, quantify significant electoral losses for the incumbent, and show that those losses were driven by voters switching from the incumbent to the new party. Three lessons emerge. First, incumbents who preside over state violence may be held electorally accountable by voters. Second, such accountability seemingly depends on the existence of credible opposition parties that can serve as a vector for disaffected voters. Where such parties do not exist, violence may create political cleavages that facilitate the formalization of opposition movements. Third, immediate proximity to violence is correlated with holding incumbents accountable.</p>
</details>

## Working Papers

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

The Limits of Local Elections Under Subnational Authoritarianism: Evidence From Nigeria (with Nick Cruz). <span class="status">Under review.</span>
<span class="links">[Draft (PDF)]({{ '/assets/pdf/papers/local-elections.pdf' | relative_url }})</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>When do local government (LG) elections fail to improve public service delivery? Using Nigeria as a case study, we develop a theory of electoral institutions that explains how unconstrained subnational executives can exploit decentralized electoral institutions to disrupt democratic accountability at the local level, thereby neutralizing the incentive to provide adequate public services. By absorbing electoral institutions into clientelistic political machines, Nigerian governors can influence both the selection of candidates and election outcomes themselves. Nigerian governors are known to illegally cancel local elections and instead directly appoint "caretaker" committees. Where local elections were held, the political party of state governors has consistently won an overwhelming majority of all LG chairmanship elections. Using data on healthcare and education outcomes from the Nigerian General Household Survey (2010–2018) and a difference-in-differences design, we find that elected local leaders perform no better than appointed ones, across various model specifications. Consequently, we conclude that the introduction of LG elections are of little significance to Nigeria's public services due to the capture of both the LG electoral process and LG funds by state governors. This research contributes to a growing literature on subnational authoritarianism, showing how decentralized electoral institutions, often promoted as vehicles to further democratization, can instead be weaponized by subnational authoritarian leaders to entrench their control.</p>
</details>
</div>

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

Fuel Subsidy Removal in Nigeria: Household Welfare and Public Opinion. <span class="status">Revise and resubmit.</span>
<span class="links">[Draft (PDF)]({{ '/assets/pdf/papers/fuel-subsidy.pdf' | relative_url }})</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>This article assesses the effects of fuel subsidy removal on Nigerian households using panel data from the Nigeria National Longitudinal Phone Survey. Employing a difference-in-differences strategy exploiting geographic variation in fuel supply infrastructure and urban--rural differences in fuel dependence, we find adverse welfare effects following the May 2023 reform. Households in states with greater fuel depot infrastructure experienced larger welfare declines. Urban households experienced greater deterioration in consumption and food security. A triple-difference specification yields results robust to confounding threats. Approximately 87 per cent of respondents disapproved of removal, citing increased prices. Absent compensatory transfers, fuel subsidy removal generates significant welfare costs concentrated among populations most dependent on formal fuel markets.</p>
</details>
</div>

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

The Man Leopard Murders and Contemporary Trust in the Nigerian Criminal Justice System (with Godwin Okore). <span class="status">Under review.</span>
<span class="links">[Draft (PDF)]({{ '/assets/pdf/papers/man-leopard.pdf' | relative_url }})</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Between 1943 and 1948, more than 200 people were killed in Calabar Province, Nigeria, by individuals disguised as leopards. The colonial administration responded with the largest police investigation in Nigerian colonial history and a public court process that resulted in the arrest of 96 suspects and the execution of 77 persons found guilty by the court. We examine whether these events generated long-term trust in criminal justice institutions. Using geolocated Afrobarometer data (rounds 3–9) matched to historical boundaries, we find that residents of Calabar Province report substantially higher trust in courts than residents of the surrounding South East region. The effect is specific to courts: placebo tests on six other outcomes of institutional trust are null. Trust in the police is elevated only within the Man Leopard zone itself, where the police investigation was concentrated. Cohort analysis shows the trust-in-courts advantage is concentrated among the 1940s and 1980s birth cohorts; and the effect is stronger among men, consistent with the gendered transmission of memory about the events. The court trial left a more durable legacy than the police investigation, suggesting that visible judicial resolution of community crises can build lasting institutional trust.</p>
</details>
</div>

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

One-Party Legislatures? Partisan Dominance in Nigeria's State Houses of Assembly. <span class="status">Under review.</span>
<span class="links">[Draft (PDF)]({{ '/assets/pdf/papers/state-legislatures.pdf' | relative_url }})</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Party-system fragmentation is conventionally interpreted as evidence of competition, and competition as the engine of accountability. This paper identifies a condition under which that inference fails. Drawing on an original dataset of 2,975 legislators across all 36 Nigerian states and three electoral cycles (2015, 2019, 2023), I document a puzzle: between 2015 and 2023 the effective number of parties in the state assemblies rose and zero-opposition chambers all but disappeared, yet the governor's party retained supermajority control of the floor. I argue that opposition fragmentation operated as a mechanism of authoritarian resilience. New parties entered divided, each lacking the patronage infrastructure to cohere into a legislative bloc, so splitting the anti-incumbent vote produced the appearance of pluralism without the substance of accountability. A governor party-switch quasi-experiment, in which the incoming party is exogenous to the assembly's composition, and a cross-carpeting analysis of returning legislators trace the mechanism. The Nigerian case extends the subnational authoritarianism framework and specifies when counting parties conceals executive capture.</p>
</details>
</div>

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

The Governor's Choice? Speaker Selection in Nigerian State Assemblies. <span class="status">Under review.</span>
<span class="links">[Draft (PDF)]({{ '/assets/pdf/papers/speaker-selection.pdf' | relative_url }})</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Legislatures elect their own presiding officers, yet these officers do not always answer to the chambers that elect them. When does the presiding officer serve the legislative majority, and when the executive? I argue that the effective principal is set by who controls the two resources legislators depend on to hold their seats: entry through candidate nomination, and the fiscal and patronage base that sustains a career. Where the executive controls both and the legislature is weakly institutionalized, the presiding officer becomes an agent of the executive. Using an original dataset covering all 36 Nigerian states across three electoral cycles, 2,975 legislators, I show that Speakers come from the governor's party almost without exception, that governors install junior members over senior co-partisans, and that where the governor's party trails in the chamber the Speaker still follows the governor. The argument generates comparable predictions for other federal democracies in which executives control nomination and intergovernmental transfers.</p>
</details>
</div>

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

Precolonial States and Development: Evidence from Agriculture in Africa (with [Aditya Dasgupta](https://sites.google.com/view/adasgupta/home?authuser=0)). <span class="status">Draft available upon request.</span>

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Low agricultural productivity is a major source of poverty in Africa, where much of the population works in agriculture, yet subsistence production and food insecurity are widespread. However, some pockets of agriculture in Africa are highly productive. In this paper, we assemble a geospatial dataset of all pre-colonial African states in existence between 1500 and 1850, and utilize remote-sensing data based on satellite imagery to show that areas (pixels) in proximity to the location of pre-colonial state capitals display higher levels of contemporary agricultural output. This relationship exists across and within countries, agro-ecological zones, and river basins. We rule out spurious correlation with spatial randomization tests. We argue that via path-dependence and spatial agglomeration effects, pre-colonial states transmitted the territorial reach that was critical for state-led agricultural modernization in the twentieth century. The findings support a growing literature linking contemporary economic development to state capacity transmitted from pre-colonial political institutions.</p>
</details>

<div class="entry" markdown="1">

The Political Economy of Colonial Treaty-Making: Evidence from British Treaties in West Africa, 1788–1901.

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>What determined which African polities received colonial treaties, and how did treaty forms map onto sovereignty outcomes? Using an original dataset of about 450 British treaties in West Africa (1788–1901) extracted from Hertslet's <em>Map of Africa by Treaty</em>, I examine the relationship between African political structures and the type of treaty imposed by British agents. I advance three core claims. First, African states that served as commercial intermediaries (middlemen) between European coastal traders and interior producers were subjected to more coercive treaty campaigns designed to eliminate their brokerage monopolies. Second, centralized polities with significant military capacity received individually negotiated treaties with more favorable terms, while smaller decentralized communities received standardized form treaties (Forms 1–10). Third, the progression of treaty forms from simple protection agreements to full territorial cession represented a deliberate strategy of incremental sovereignty erosion, in which initial "protection" clauses contained embedded provisions enabling progressive expansion of colonial control. These findings contribute to ongoing debates about the origins of colonial boundaries and the mechanisms through which imperial claims were constructed and legitimized.</p>
</details>
